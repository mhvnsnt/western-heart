# WRITING CRAFT GUIDE — Western Heart

*A practical reference for writing character bios in the Western Heart universe. For human writers and AI agents alike. The goal: stop producing slop, start producing writing that belongs in Tong-Nou.*

---

## How to use this document

- **Section 1** diagnoses the mechanical habits that make AI writing read as "slop." Read it first. Every rule here maps to a specific failure mode.
- **Section 2** gives you the Japanese aesthetic traditions the universe is built on. These aren't decoration — they're the operating system.
- **Section 3** covers the spiritual and philosophical frameworks (Buddhist, Shinto, bushido) that feed the themes of sin, honor, trial, and purification.
- **Section 4** is the actionable style guide: the canon format, voice patterns, and concrete rules with before/after examples.
- **Section 5** is a mythological sourcebook — a palette of folkloric traditions to draw from so characters feel rooted in *something*.

**Standing laws this guide serves:**
- ALL characters are adults (or divine beings, animals, demons, creatures). No minors, ever, unless the owner explicitly authorizes it.
- All writing must be grounded in CANON.md. If it's not in canon, don't write it like it's canon.
- Tekken fighter names stay OUT of canon writing. Moveset sources go in technical files only.

## Section 1: Why AI Writing Reads as Slop

### 1.1 The one-trait trap

**The tell:** A character has one trait, and the bio says it eight different ways.

> ❌ SLOP: "She was fast in life — a runner, a messenger. She's been moving ever since. She fights with relentless speed. She's not fighting opponents, she's fighting stillness. Brash, breathless, always in motion. Talks fast. Fights faster. She bounces on the balls of her feet. During introductions. During fights. During win poses."

Count the ideas: fast. That's one idea, said nine times.

**The fix — the three-dimension rule:** Every character needs at least three distinct dimensions that create tension with each other. Not three adjectives — three *forces*.

> ✅ GROUNDED: "In life she carried messages between villages — sealed letters, mostly. She never read them. That was the job: carry, deliver, don't ask. The call found her on a road between two places, a satchel full of other people's words on her back. She's never set the satchel down. She's never opened it."

Now she has: (1) a job with a rule (carry, don't read), (2) a physical burden (the satchel), (3) a contradiction (carries messages she's never read). Three dimensions. They pull against each other. That's a character.

**Diagnostic test:** Cover the character's name. Can you still tell who it is from any single paragraph? If every paragraph reduces to the same adjective, you have one trait, not a character.

### 1.2 Over-explanation (telling, then telling again)

**The tell:** AI states an idea, then restates it as if the reader didn't get it.

> ❌ SLOP: "She's the only one who makes her laugh — a short, surprised sound she doesn't seem to expect. She's the only one who can calm him when the hunger takes over."

> ❌ SLOP: "The acid isn't a technique. It's what leaks out when she stops holding it in." (Followed two sentences later by: "The acid is chemistry. The grudge is what's eating through her.")

**The fix:** State once. Trust the reader. The canon's best lines are never explained:

> ✅ CANON: "Maybe there is no without it." (Skull's paint — no follow-up. No explanation. It lands because it's left alone.)

> ✅ CANON: "He thinks the paint was his idea. He thinks the mission was his choice." (No "this shows his self-deception." The reader feels it.)

**Rule:** If you've said it, stop. If you're tempted to rephrase it "more clearly," delete the rephrase. Clarity in this universe comes from precision, not repetition.

### 1.3 American colloquialisms in a non-American universe

**The tell:** Dialogue and philosophy quotes that sound like they were written in a Los Angeles writers' room.

> ❌ SLOP: "You don't get to tell me to calm down."
> ❌ SLOP: "You can't hit what already left."
> ❌ SLOP: "Everyone pays. I'm just faster at collecting."
> ❌ SLOP: "Kneel or be knelt."

These are bumper stickers, not philosophy. They belong on a t-shirt, not in Tong-Nou.

**The fix:** Study the canon's actual fighting philosophy quotes. They're aphoristic, paradoxical, imagistic:

> ✅ CANON: "The hold is honest. Everything else is negotiation." (Pantera)
> ✅ CANON: "The straight line is a lie. Everything stumbles." (Zui)
> ✅ CANON: "Mercy is a luxury of the unbroken." (Skull)
> ✅ CANON: "The cut is the prayer. The wound is the answer." (Phantom)
> ✅ CANON: "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm—" *[kick]*. (Waterfall)

**What makes these work:**
- They're short (under 12 words, usually under 10).
- They contain a paradox or inversion (honest hold vs. negotiation, prayer vs. wound, apology vs. kick).
- They sound like something carved in stone, not said in a green room.
- No slang. No contractions-as-attitude. No pop-culture cadence.

**Test:** Read the quote aloud. If it sounds like a movie trailer tagline, rewrite it. If it sounds like something you'd find on a temple wall, keep it.

### 1.4 Rhetorical templates and the "rule of three" padding

**The tell:** AI loves tricolons (lists of three), "not only X but also Y," and balanced oppositions. Individually harmless. In density, they scream "generated."

> ❌ SLOP: "She's warm, sharp, tired." / "Loud, defensive, fiercely loyal." / "Quiet, observant, dry."

Three adjectives, comma-separated, as a complete personality description. This appears in nearly every AI bio. It's not wrong — it's *weightless*. It tells you nothing you couldn't get from a character-creation slider.

**The fix:** Replace adjective lists with *behavior*.

> ✅ CANON: "She's the only fighter who visits the losers after matches. Not to gloat — to check. She brings water. She doesn't talk." (Kinpo — you learn more from this than from any adjective list.)

> ✅ CANON: "He toasts them before every fight: 'Still learning, you bastards.'" (Zui — one action reveals the entire relationship.)

**Rule:** Never describe personality with three adjectives. Show one specific behavior that implies the personality. If you can't think of the behavior, you don't know the character yet.

### 1.5 False specificity (inventing instead of grounding)

**The tell:** AI invents proper nouns — places, systems, organizations — instead of using the ones that exist.

> ❌ SLOP: "She runs the Velvet Room — neutral ground club." / "She invented the Nine-Soul Meter." / "Her family's soul-binding crimes."

The Velvet Room doesn't exist in canon. The Nine-Soul Meter already exists (as the Soul Surge mechanic). "Soul-binding crimes" is invented. Every invented proper noun is a thread pulling the reader *out* of the universe.

**The fix:** Before writing any proper noun, check: is this in CANON.md? The canon has:
- Five Lands with names, elements, and specific details (the Fire Tower of Time, the five glowing rooms of the Helix Palace, the Sha-mo Desert)
- The Phantom Marketplace, the Trader, the Eyeball Mountains
- The Octagonal Shrine Par-fang, the Central Mountain
- The nine souls with kanji and natures
- The Reincarnation mechanic, the Soul Surge, the katana

**Rule:** New proper nouns require owner approval. Existing proper nouns are your palette. When in doubt, describe the thing without naming it — "the marketplace" is fine if the Phantom Marketplace is too specific for the moment.

### 1.6 Explaining the metaphor (killing yugen)

**The tell:** AI writes an evocative image, then explains what it means.

> ❌ SLOP: "Her shadow doesn't always match her body. Fighters have seen wings, tails, antlers — there for a frame, gone. She pretends not to notice them noticing. The beasts pretend harder."

That last sentence explains the image. It's the AI not trusting the image to work.

> ✅ Better: "Her shadow doesn't always match her body. Fighters have seen wings, tails, antlers — there for a frame, gone."

Stop there. The reader feels the wrongness. The explanation adds nothing.

**This is the yugen principle** (see Section 2): depth that is suggested, not stated. The canon does this constantly:

> ✅ CANON: "The wounds close between rounds. The pain doesn't." (Phantom — no explanation of what this means. It doesn't need one.)

**Rule:** Write the image. Delete the sentence that explains the image. If the image can't stand alone, the image is wrong — don't prop it up with explanation.

### 1.7 The assistant voice leak

**The tell:** Phrases like "here's the thing," "let's unpack this," "it's worth noting that," "in other words." These are the AI talking to you, not the universe talking to the reader.

**The fix:** The canon never addresses the reader. It never says "as you can see" or "it's important to understand." It states. It describes. It leaves.

**Rule:** No meta-commentary. No "what this means is." No rhetorical questions to the reader. The bio is a document *from* the universe, not *about* it.

### 1.8 Uniform rhythm (every paragraph the same shape)

**The tell:** Every section is 3-4 sentences. Every sentence is medium length. The bio reads like it was poured into a mold.

**The fix:** Vary ruthlessly. The canon mixes:
- Single-sentence paragraphs for impact: "Her name is the shape of her sorrow: it doesn't stop."
- Fragments: "Not to gloat — to check."
- Long, winding sentences for backstory, then a short punch: "She arrived in the Western Heart still carrying them. She's never set the satchel down."

**Rule:** Read the bio aloud. If you can predict the rhythm of the next paragraph, break the rhythm.

## Section 2: Japanese Literary and Aesthetic Traditions

These aren't set dressing. They're the operating system the universe runs on. The owner's north star says: "the Oni and Yokai thing, along with the whole Eastern Mind, Tong-Nou thing." That means the writing should *think* the way these traditions think.

### 2.1 Mono no aware (物の哀れ) — the pathos of things

**What it is:** A gentle sadness at the transience of things. Not grief — *awareness*. The cherry blossom is beautiful *because* it falls. The feeling is in the passing, not the thing.

**How it applies to character writing:**
- Every fighter in the Western Heart dies and returns (the Reincarnation). That's mono no aware as game mechanic — beauty in the cycle, not despite it.
- Don't write characters who rage against impermanence. Write characters who *notice* it.
- The signature detail should often carry this: Zui's blank tattoo patch ("for the night he hasn't survived yet") is pure mono no aware — a space held for a future loss.

**In practice:** When writing a character's relationship to the trial, don't make it about winning. Make it about *what passes through them* while they fight. Waterfall doesn't fight to win — she holds the grief as it passes. That's mono no aware as character.

### 2.2 Wabi-sabi (侘寂) — beauty in imperfection

**What it is:** The aesthetic of the cracked teabowl, the moss-covered stone. Things are beautiful not despite their damage but *through* it. Nothing is perfect, nothing is permanent, nothing is complete.

**How it applies:**
- Characters should have cracks. Not tragic backstories — *cracks*. The specific, weathered detail.
- Skull's paint that wouldn't come off for three days. That's wabi-sabi — the imperfection becoming the truth.
- Avoid pristine characters. Avoid characters whose damage is purely decorative. The crack should be where the light gets in — it should *do* something in the bio.

**In practice:** When you write Body/Mind/Soul, the friction between the three layers IS the wabi-sabi. The character is a cracked vessel. Don't smooth the cracks. Name them.

### 2.3 Ma (間) — negative space as presence

**What it is:** Emptiness is not absence. The pause in Noh theater, the blank space in a painting, the silence between drum beats — these are *active*. They mean.

**How it applies:**
- What you don't say is as important as what you say. (See 1.6: don't explain the metaphor.)
- Phantom's relationships section: "**No attachments.** Phantom does not form bonds. He forms observations." The emptiness IS the content.
- In dialogue: the best lines have space around them. "Maybe there is no without it." — the period is doing work.

**In practice:**
- Leave gaps in backstory. Not every question needs an answer. ("No one has mentioned it to her face." — the reader fills the silence.)
- Use single-sentence paragraphs as pauses. They're the ma of prose.
- If a section feels "thin," don't pad it. The thinness might be the point.

### 2.4 Yugen (幽玄) — mysterious depth

**What it is:** A profound, mysterious sense of beauty. What is suggested but not shown. The moon behind clouds. Zeami (founder of Noh) called it the highest aesthetic ideal — "to watch the sun sink behind a flower-clad hill, to wander on in a huge forest without thought of return."

**How it applies:**
- The best canon lines operate on yugen: "The shrine stones go quiet when she passes." *Why* do they go quiet? Not explained. The mystery is the meaning.
- Don't resolve every tension. The Body/Mind/Soul friction should *remain* frictional — not solved by the end of the bio.

**In practice:** Every bio should have at least one line that the reader can't fully parse but *feels*. If everything is explicable, there's no depth.

### 2.5 Kishotenketsu (起承転結) — four-act structure without conflict

**What it is:** The East Asian narrative structure: ki (introduction), sho (development), ten (twist/turn), ketsu (reconciliation). Unlike Western three-act structure, it doesn't require conflict. The ten is not a complication — it's a *recontextualization*. What looked ordinary becomes charged.

**The four moves:**
1. **Ki (起):** Introduce the elements. Plain. Factual.
2. **Sho (承):** Develop. Continue. Nothing "happens" — details accumulate.
3. **Ten (転):** The turn. A new element, a revelation, a shift in perspective. Not a plot twist — a *meaning* twist.
4. **Ketsu (結):** Reconciliation. The earlier elements are re-read in light of the turn.

**How it applies to backstory writing:**

> **Ki:** She carried messages between villages. Sealed letters. Never read them.
> **Sho:** The call found her on the road. She arrived with the satchel. She's never set it down. The Reincarnation has given her new faces. The satchel survives every death.
> **Ten:** She's never opened it. She doesn't know whose words she's been carrying.
> **Ketsu:** Whatever's inside has been waiting a very long time. (The earlier details — the job, the rule, the satchel — are all re-read through this lens.)

The ten isn't "and then she was attacked." It's "she's never opened it" — a quiet fact that rewrites everything before it.

**In practice:** Structure every backstory as kishotenketsu, not as hero's-journey. The bio doesn't need a climax. It needs a turn that makes the reader re-read.

### 2.6 Haiku aesthetics — brevity, image, the cutting word

**What it is:** Haiku works through juxtaposition of two images separated by a *kireji* (cutting word). No explanation. The meaning lives in the gap.

> Old pond / frog jumps in / sound of water. (Basho)

Three images. No commentary. The philosophy is in the arrangement.

**How it applies:**
- Fighting philosophy quotes should work like haiku: two images, a cut, no explanation.
- "The cut is the prayer. The wound is the answer." — Image (cut/prayer), cut (period), image (wound/answer). The reader bridges them.
- Signature details should be haiku-like: one concrete image that implies everything.

**In practice:** When writing a philosophy quote, try to make it work as two images with a gap. If you need a third clause to explain the first two, delete the third clause.

### 2.7 Noh theater — the ghost protagonist and the witness

**What it is:** In *mugen noh* (supernatural Noh), the *shite* (protagonist) is often a ghost or spirit. The *waki* (secondary character, usually a traveling priest) encounters them, asks questions, and *witnesses* their story. The ghost performs their truth through dance. The priest doesn't fix anything — they listen.

**How it applies:**
- Many Western Heart characters ARE Noh ghosts: dead, lingering, performing their nature.
- The bio writer is the *waki* — the witness. Not the explainer. Not the therapist. The one who listens and records.
- The "Why they think they're here" section is the ghost's testimony. The bio doesn't correct it — it lets the gap between belief and truth sit there, like the Noh audience sits with the ghost's delusion.

**In practice:** Write as the waki. Observe. Record. Don't diagnose. The character's self-understanding may be wrong — that's fine. The Noh ghost is always wrong about something. That's what makes them a ghost.

### 2.8 Zuihitsu (随筆) — fragmentary writing

**What it is:** "Following the brush." Fragmentary, associative essays (Sei Shonagon's *Pillow Book*). Lists, observations, juxtapositions without forced connection.

**How it applies:**
- The Relationships section works best as zuihitsu — discrete observations, not a unified theory of the character's social life.
- "Pantera: Mutual understanding. Both know violence as vocation, not temper." — one brushstroke. Next.
- Don't force relationships into an arc. Let them accumulate like pillow-book entries.

## Section 3: Spiritual and Philosophical Frameworks

The universe's themes — sin, honor, justice, righteousness, trial, purification — come from real traditions. Use them as *lenses*, not lectures. The goal is characters who *embody* these frameworks, not characters who *explain* them.

### 3.1 Buddhism: samsara, the bardo, and purification through suffering

**Samsara (the cycle):** The endless cycle of death and rebirth. Beings are trapped until they achieve liberation.

*Western Heart already has this:* the Reincarnation mechanic — fighters die and return with new faces. This IS samsara as game design. When writing, lean into it:
- The Reincarnation isn't just respawn. It's the philosophical engine. Every death is a chance to be purified. Every return is a choice (or failure) to change.
- Characters who don't learn across deaths are tragic in the Buddhist sense — trapped in the cycle.
- "Being purified of" is the bodhisattva path in miniature: what attachment is the trial burning away?

**The bardo (intermediate state):** In Tibetan Buddhism, the bardo is the state between death and rebirth — a liminal space where the consciousness encounters visions.

*Western Heart already has this:* the moment between rounds, the space where the new face is chosen. The Phantom Marketplace has bardo qualities — liminal, transactional, between.

*Writing application:* Characters who exist *between* — not fully in the trial, not outside it — are bardo beings. Birodo (who doesn't walk the ladder), the Trader (who was there before and will be there after). Write their liminality, not their backstory.

**Purification through suffering:** Not punishment — *refinement*. Fire doesn't punish gold; it purifies it.

*This is the trial's core thesis:* "Tong-Nou was not devouring souls. Tong-Nou was *purifying* them. The trial was healing all along."

*Writing application:* "Being purified of" should never read as punishment. It's always the trial *removing* something to reveal what's underneath. Skull isn't being punished for violence — the trial is stripping the *mission* off the violence to reveal the pure force underneath. That's Buddhist purification: removing the attachment, not the energy.

### 3.2 Shinto: kegare, harae, and kami

**Kegare (穢れ — impurity):** Not "sin" in the Western sense. Kegare is *depletion* — a state where life energy has dried up. Death, disease, and wrongdoing all cause kegare. It's not moral judgment; it's spiritual physics.

*Writing application:* When a character is "corrupted" or "stained," think kegare, not sin. It's not that they did wrong — it's that something is *drained* in them. Ryusan's corrosion isn't evil; it's depletion. The trial restores by replenishing, not by forgiving.

**Harae (祓 — purification) and Oharae (the Great Purification):** Ritual purification, performed regularly. The Oharae was a national "reset" — all accumulated impurities extinguished, twice a year, for over a thousand years.

*Western Heart already has this:* the trial itself is harae at cosmic scale. Each fight is a purification ritual. The Reincarnation is the reset.

*Writing application:* Think of fights as rituals, not contests. The pre-fight behaviors (Skull's paint, Zui's toast, Megami straightening the ground) are harae — personal purification rites. Give characters ritual behaviors, not just "warm-ups."

**Kami (神):** Spirits/gods that inhabit natural things — trees, rivers, stones, places. Not omnipotent; *present*.

*Writing application:* The Five Lands aren't just arenas — they're kami-dense. The Tree of Life holds souls like fruit. The shrine stones react to passersby. Write the environment as *aware*. "The walls ripple slightly less when she's near" — that's kami logic. The world notices.

### 3.3 Bushido: the seven virtues as character axes

The seven virtues aren't a checklist — they're *tensions*. A character can embody one while violating another. That's where drama lives.

| Virtue | Kanji | Meaning | Character axis |
|--------|-------|---------|----------------|
| Gi | 義 | Rectitude, justice | Does the character know what's right? Do they do it? |
| Yu | 勇 | Courage | Is their bravery in service of righteousness, or just violence? (Bushido distinguishes: courage only counts if exercised for a just cause.) |
| Jin | 仁 | Benevolence | Can someone who kills also show mercy? Where's the line? |
| Rei | 礼 | Respect, courtesy | How do they treat enemies? The defeated? The dead? |
| Makoto | 誠 | Honesty, sincerity | Do their words match their actions? What do they lie about? |
| Meiyo | 誉 | Honor | What would they die for? What *have* they died for? |
| Chu | 忠 | Loyalty | To whom? What happens when loyalties conflict? |

*Writing application:* Don't assign a character "the honorable one." Instead, put two virtues in conflict:
- Demonio has *rei* (bows to everyone) but is being purified of guilt — his *makoto* (honesty with himself) is broken.
- Skull has a warrior's *gi* (he punishes the guilty) but it's self-appointed — the trial is asking whether his justice is real or costume.
- Zui abandoned the monastery (violated *chu* to his order) for a deeper honesty (found *makoto* in the bottle).

**The Suzuki principle** (from D.T. Suzuki's *Zen and Japanese Culture*): "The sword has a double office: to destroy what opposes the will of its owner, and to sacrifice all impulses that arise from self-preservation." Every fighter in the Western Heart holds this double edge. The question isn't whether they fight — it's whether the sword is turned outward, inward, or both.

### 3.4 How games have done this (without preaching)

**Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice** — Buddhist horror of immortality. The game's central horror isn't death — it's *not being able to die*. The Rejuvenating Waters grant eternal life, but it corrupts. This is samsara as nightmare: the cycle you can't escape. *Lesson:* Pick one Buddhist concept and make it *literal* in the world. Don't explain it. Let the world embody it.

**Ghost of Tsushima** — Jin's conflict between bushido (honor, direct confrontation) and survival (stealth, pragmatism). The game never lectures about bushido — it *stages* the conflict. Every stealth kill is a bushido violation the player feels. *Lesson:* Embody philosophical conflict in *action*, not dialogue. A character's virtue should be tested by what they *do*, not what they say.

**Nioh** — Yokai as moral correction. The yokai aren't random monsters — they manifest from human failings (greed, rage, attachment). *Lesson:* Connect the supernatural to the moral. If a character has a strange power or condition, root it in a human failing or virtue. The weirdness should *mean* something.

**Dark Souls (general principle)** — Item descriptions as zuihitsu. Fragments, not exposition. The lore is in the gaps. *Lesson:* Trust the fragment. A single line ("He thinks the paint was his idea") does more than a paragraph of psychology.

### 3.5 The non-preachy rule

**Never have a character explain the philosophy.** Zui quotes sutras he claims to have forgotten — that's character, not lecture. The moment a bio says "In Buddhism, this means...", you've broken the universe.

**Instead:**
- Show through ritual (Skull's paint, Zui's toast, Megami's sweeping).
- Show through object (Tobi's satchel, Zui's blank tattoo patch).
- Show through habit (Pantera's tail, Pond's single blink).
- Show through the world's reaction (shrine stones going quiet, walls rippling less).

The philosophy is in the *behavior*, not the *exposition*. The reader should feel the Buddhism without the word "Buddhism" appearing.

## Section 4: Writing Style Guide for Western Heart

### 4.1 The canon format

Every character bio follows this structure. Don't deviate. Don't add sections. Don't rename them.

1. **Name — The [X] Soul** (+ vessel/category line)
2. **Backstory.** (kishotenketsu structure: setup, development, turn, reconciliation)
3. **Personality.** (One behavior, not three adjectives. See 1.4.)
4. **Fighting philosophy.** (One quote. See 4.3.)
5. **Relationships.** (Zuihitsu fragments. See 4.4.)
6. **Element.** (One of the Five Lands + why *this* fighter belongs there.)
7. **Body / Mind / Soul.** (Three layers with FRICTION. See 4.5.)
8. **Why [X] thinks they're here.** (Always includes the gap. See 4.6.)
9. **Being purified of.** (The inversion. See 4.7.)
10. **Signature detail.** (One concrete image. See 4.8.)
11. **Owner-locked.** (Technical constraints, chat-canon, naming decisions.)

### 4.2 Voice patterns from existing canon

**Sentence-level patterns to internalize:**
- Short declaratives: "The paint goes on before every fight."
- Em-dash pivots: "Not to gloat — to check."
- Parenthetical undercuts: "He thinks the paint was his idea."
- Negative definitions: "Phantom does not form bonds. He forms observations."
- Present tense for eternal truths, past tense for backstory events.

**What the voice NEVER does:**
- Addresses the reader ("as you can see," "it's worth noting").
- Uses slang or contemporary colloquialisms ("gonna," "wanna," "kinda").
- Explains jokes or images.
- Uses exclamation points (the universe doesn't shout in prose).
- Hedging ("sort of," "kind of," "somewhat") — commit or cut.

### 4.3 Fighting philosophy quotes — the rules

1. **Under 12 words.** Under 10 is better.
2. **Must contain a paradox, inversion, or juxtaposition.** Two images with a gap.
3. **No slang.** No contractions used for attitude. No pop cadence.
4. **Must sound carved, not spoken.** Temple wall, not green room.
5. **Must be specific to THIS character.** If it could belong to anyone, it's wrong.

**Before/after:**

> ❌ "You don't get to tell me to calm down." (Could be anyone. Sounds like an argument.)
> ✅ "Fire purifies. Even wildfire." (Specific: her acid, her rage, the trial's method. Paradox: destruction as cleansing.)

> ❌ "You can't hit what already left." (Bumper sticker. Generic speedster line.)
> ✅ "The message doesn't stop for the storm." (Specific: she's a carrier. Image: message/storm. Implies duty beyond obstacle.)

> ❌ "Kneel or be knelt." (Threat, not philosophy. Any tyrant could say it.)
> ✅ "A crown is heaviest when there's no head beneath it." (Paradox: the weight of absent authority. Specific to a fallen royal.)

### 4.4 Relationships that do double work

Every relationship entry must reveal **character** AND **cosmology**. If it only does one, rewrite it.

> ❌ Single work (character only): "She's friends with Awa Pop. They get along."
> ❌ Single work (cosmology only): "The shrine stones are old."
> ✅ Double work: "The shrine stones go quiet when she passes. Megami sweeps harder." (Reveals: Chatur disturbs the sacred order + the stones are aware + Megami notices.)

**The zuihitsu rule:** Each relationship is one brushstroke. 1-3 sentences. Don't connect them into a narrative. Let them accumulate.

**Relationship types that work in this universe:**
- **Recognition:** Two characters see something in each other (Pantera/Skull: "Both know violence as vocation, not temper.")
- **Disturbance:** One character unsettles another (Waterfall/Skull: "She weeps for the people he's punished, and he doesn't know what to do with that.")
- **Witness:** One character observes another without intervening (Phantom/Waterfall: "Watches her. She feels him even when he's elsewhere.")
- **Debt:** Unspoken obligation (Kinpo visiting losers with water — never explained, always done.)
- **Mirror:** The other character reflects what this one can't see (Zui/Pantera: "Finds him exhausting. Has never said so. The tail twitches when Zui talks.")

### 4.5 Body / Mind / Soul — the friction rule

The three layers must be in **tension**. If they agree, there's no character.

> ✅ CANON (Skull): "BODY: Bryan's brutal frame, every hit meant to end. MIND: judgment — he sees the world as the guilty and the soon-to-be-punished. SOUL: RETSU, pure violence. **The friction:** RETSU is *force*, but Skull's mind has made it *moral*. Violence doesn't punish. Skulls do."

The friction IS the character. Body wants X, mind wants Y, soul IS Z, and they don't align.

> ✅ CANON (Zui): "The body is disciplined. The mind is free. The soul *moves wrong* — and Zui is the only vessel who *agrees* with his soul."

Even agreement is framed as remarkable *because* friction is the norm.

- BODY: [Physical description + what the body wants/do

## Section 5: Mythological Sourcebook

*A palette of folkloric traditions. Not a costume shop — a set of ontologies. When you need a character to feel rooted in* something*, reach for a specific folkloric logic and let it texture the writing.*

**How to use this section:** Don't copy myths. Don't name-drop beings as decoration. Instead, borrow the *logic* — the way a tradition understands transformation, debt, hunger, or justice — and let it shape a character's nature. "She carries a rokurokubi's curse" should mean something specific about how she lives, not just sound cool.

**A note on respect:** Some of these traditions are living religions, not just stories. The guide flags where casual use is inappropriate. When in doubt, borrow the *structural idea* (a hunger that can't be fed, a debt to the dead) rather than the specific sacred figure.

### 5.1 Japanese traditions

#### Yokai — the logic of imbalance

Yokai aren't "monsters." They're what happens when the world goes out of balance. Many exist *to correct imbalance*, even if the method is terrible. A yokai is never random — it's a response.

**Using yokai logic:** Give a character a *trigger condition*. They don't act arbitrarily; they activate when something specific is violated. The specificity is what makes it feel folkloric instead of generic.

| Being | Core nature | Thematic material | Character texture (not costume) |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Kitsune** (fox spirit) | Shapeshifter, illusionist. Serves Inari or goes rogue. Gains tails (and power) with age. Can be protector or deceiver — sometimes both. | Loyalty vs. deception. The long game. What centuries of life do to love. | A character whose kindness might be a centuries-long con — or whose cruelty might be protection. The reader should never be sure which. Don't give them fox ears; give them *uncertain motives*. |
| **Tengu** (mountain spirit) | Proud warrior-monks of the mountains. Masters of swordsmanship. Punish arrogance — especially in warriors. Originally bird-demons, evolved into ascetic teachers. | Pride as the fatal flaw. The teacher who humbles. Martial mastery as spiritual discipline. | A character who appears to test fighters' arrogance. Doesn't fight to win — fights to *teach*. Leaves the arrogant broken and the humble stronger. The tengu doesn't hate you; it's disappointed in you. |
| **Kappa** (river spirit) | Water-dweller. Bound by etiquette — if you bow, it bows back, spilling the water in its head-dish and weakening it. Obsessed with courtesy and cucumbers. Deadly but rule-bound. | The power of protocol. Even monsters have codes. Politeness as weapon and weakness. | A character who *cannot* break a rule of conduct, even when it costs them. Their strength and their vulnerability are the same thing: the code. Write the code, not the turtle shell. |
| **Rokurokubi** (long-necked woman) | Appears human by day; neck stretches at night. Often doesn't know her own nature — the transformation happens while she sleeps. | The divided self. The part of you that acts without your knowledge. What you are when you're not watching. | A character with a waking self and a sleeping self that do different things. She doesn't remember what her neck does at night. The horror isn't the neck — it's the *not knowing*. |
| **Jorogumo** (binding bride) | Spider spirit, 400 years old, takes the form of a beautiful woman. Seduces, binds, devours. Patience as predation. | Desire as trap. The long seduction. What 400 years of waiting does to hunger. | A character whose affection is indistinguishable from hunting. Not cruel — *patient*. The terror is in how long she's willing to wait. Don't write a spider-woman; write someone for whom love and consumption are the same verb. |
| **Tsukumogami** (object spirits) | Everyday objects that awaken after 100 years of existence. Umbrellas, lanterns, sandals — given spirit by age and neglect. | The dignity of discarded things. What abandonment creates. Objects have memory. | A character who was *used* — a tool, a weapon, a vessel — and developed a self from the neglect. Bitter not at being used, but at being *thrown away*. The 100-year rule: spirit comes from duration, not drama. |
| **Yuki-onna** (snow woman) | Beautiful, cold, lethal. Appears in snowstorms. Sometimes shows mercy — but the mercy always has a condition. | Beauty as death. Mercy with terms. The cold that preserves and kills. | A character whose kindness is real but *conditional*. She'll spare you if you promise never to speak of her. The promise is the point — she needs to be forgotten to survive being remembered. |
| **Bakeneko / Tanuki** (shapeshifters) | Cats and raccoon-dogs that transform. Mischievous, not evil. Tanuki are jolly tricksters; bakeneko can be vengeful. Both blur the line between animal and person. | Play as worldview. The trickster who isn't cruel, just *amused*. Identity as performance. | A character who treats the trial as a game — not because they're shallow, but because play is their ontology. They don't understand why everyone is so serious about dying. This is genuinely destabilizing to other fighters. |

#### Oni — the logic of punishment

Oni are horned enforcers, often depicted with iron clubs, tiger-skin loincloths. They're sent from hell to punish the wicked. But they're not evil — they're *functionaries*. The punishment is the point, not the cruelty.

**Using oni logic:** A character who punishes — not from anger, but from *office*. They don't enjoy it. They don't question it. The horror is the bureaucracy of damnation. (Skull has oni-adjacent energy: "He thinks the mission was his choice.")

#### Kami — the logic of presence

Kami aren't gods in the Western sense. They're *presences* — in trees, rivers, stones, places. They're not omnipotent; they're *local*. They respond to attention and neglect.

**Using kami logic:** The Western Heart's Five Lands are kami-dense. Write the environment as aware — not sentient in a human way, but *responsive*. The shrine stones going quiet. The walls rippling less. This is kami logic: the world notices.

#### Yurei and Onryo — the logic of the unresolved

Yurei are restless dead. Onryo are the vengeful subset — formed from betrayal, rage, or sorrow so intense it warps the soul. The onryo doesn't haunt randomly; it haunts *specifically*. It has an address.

**Using onryo logic:** A character motivated by a specific unresolved wrong. Not generic anger — *addressed* anger. The onryo knows exactly who wronged them and exactly what is owed. This is more terrifying than rage because it's *precise*.

#### Shikigami — the logic of the servant spirit

Shikigami are spirits bound to serve an onmyoji (practitioner). They're extensions of will — but folklore warns they can develop their own agendas.

**Using shikigami logic:** A character who serves — willingly or bound — and the tension of the servant developing a self. The question isn't whether they'll rebel; it's whether the master will notice the servant has started *wanting* things.

### 5.2 American traditions

#### The wendigo — the logic of insatiable hunger

From Algonquian traditions: the wendigo is not a monster to be hunted but a *condition*. A human who gives in to greed, selfishness, or desperation — especially in winter, especially when community breaks down — *becomes* the wendigo. Emaciated, always starving, heart of ice. The more it eats, the hungrier it gets.

**Thematic material:** Greed as transformation. The community's immune response — the wendigo story exists to enforce the value that the group's needs come before the individual's. Excess as self-destruction.

**Character texture:** Not a hungry monster — a character whose *wanting* has become their nature. They've consumed and consumed and it hasn't filled the hole. The tragedy: they remember being human. They remember when enough was enough. Write the memory of satiety, not the hunger. That's what makes it American folklore instead of a generic monster.

**Respect note:** The wendigo comes from living Algonquian traditions. Use the *structural idea* (hunger that grows with feeding, greed as transformation) with care. Don't use it as a throwaway monster name.

#### The thunderbird — the logic of sky power

Across many Native American traditions: a massive bird whose wings create thunder, whose eyes flash lightning. A being of the upper world, associated with power, protection, and cosmic order. Not a god to worship — a *force* to respect.

**Thematic material:** Power that doesn't negotiate. The sky doesn't argue with the ground. Protection through overwhelming presence.

**Character texture:** A character whose power is *atmospheric* — they change the room by entering it. Not through intimidation (that's human); through *scale*. Like weather. You don't fight weather. You endure it or you don't.

#### Folk heroes — the logic of labor as dignity

**John Henry:** The steel-driving man who raced a steam drill and won — then died with his hammer in his hand. The story isn't about winning; it's about *what winning costs* and whether the cost was worth it.

**Thematic material:** Dignity through labor. The human against the machine. Victory as tragedy — he proved his worth and it killed him.

**Character texture:** A character who measures themselves by *work*, not by winning. They don't fight for the katana; they fight because the fighting *is* the work. The trial is just the current job site. What would break them isn't losing — it's being told the work doesn't matter.

**High John the Conqueror:** Trickster spirit from Black American hoodoo tradition. A figure of resistance, wit over force, survival through cleverness. He wins by being smarter, not stronger.

**Thematic material:** Resistance as creativity. The trickster who can't be caught because he was never really there.

**Character texture:** A character who has *never* won a fair fight and never intends to. Their power is in the angle, the misdirection, the thing you didn't see. Not a coward — a strategist whose ancestors survived by being uncatchable.

#### Frontier figures — the logic of scale

**Paul Bunyan:** The giant lumberjack whose exploits explain the landscape — he *made* the Grand Canyon, the Great Lakes. Tall-tale logic: the world looks the way it does because someone enormous passed through.

**Thematic material:** Myth as geography. The idea that the land *remembers* giant actions.

**Character texture:** A character whose past actions have *shaped* the Western Heart's geography. Not "they're big" — "the arena looks like that because of what they did." The landscape as biography.

#### ⚠️ The skinwalker — NOT for casual use

From Navajo (Diné) tradition: the skinwalker (*yee naaldlooshii* — "by means of it, it goes on all fours") is a deeply serious spiritual concept. It refers to a person who has deliberately corrupted spiritual power for harm. In Navajo culture, these stories are **not told casually** and are treated with gravity and respect.

**This guide's position:** Do NOT use skinwalkers as character flavor. The structural idea underneath — power corrupted by choice, the shaman who chose harm — can inform a character's *moral* logic, but the specific figure belongs to a living tradition that asks outsiders not to play with it. Respect that.

### 5.3 Yoruba tradition (West Africa / Atlantic diaspora)

The orisha are not distant gods — they're *forces* that manifest in human life. Each has a domain, a personality, specific foods, specific taboos. They're complex: Oshun is love *and* has a destructive temper. Shango is justice *and* lashes out. This moral complexity is the point.

| Orisha | Domain | Thematic material | Character texture |
|--------|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Ogun** | Iron, war, labor, roads, truth | The worker-warrior. Ogun clears the path — with a machete. Technology as sacred. The oath sworn on iron cannot be broken. | A character whose word is *literally* binding — they swore on iron and the iron remembers. Their labor is worship. They don't fight for glory; they fight because the road needs clearing. |
| **Shango** | Thunder, justice, fire, virility, dance | Justice as *performance*. Shango doesn't just punish — he *arrives*. The double-headed axe cuts both ways. A king who became a god through sheer force of presence. | A character whose sense of justice is theatrical and absolute. They don't investigate; they *declare*. The danger: their justice is real, but so is their temper. Both come from the same fire. |
| **Oya** | Wind, storms, transformation, the dead | The storm as change. Oya guards the cemetery gates — she stands *between* the living and the dead. Sudden, total transformation. | A character who *is* the turning point. When they arrive, things change — completely, irreversibly. They don't cause change; they *are* the moment of change. Associated with thresholds: doors, gates, the instant before. |
| **Eshu / Elegba** | Crossroads, messages, chance, trickery | The trickster at the crossroads. Eshu opens the road — but which road? He delivers messages but might *edit* them. Chance as divine force. | A character who controls *access* — to places, to information, to opportunities. Not powerful in the fighting sense; powerful in the *gatekeeping* sense. Every deal goes through them. Every message is slightly... adjusted. |
| **Oshun** | Rivers, love, fertility, sweetness — and a terrifying temper | Love as power. Oshun's sweetness is *strategic*; her rage is *cataclysmic*. The river gives life and drowns. | A character whose gentleness is real and whose fury is apocalyptic — and both are the same person. Don't write "sweet but dangerous" as a twist; write it as *one thing*. The river doesn't have moods. The river *is* both. |
| **Yemoja** | Ocean, motherhood, protection | The mother as ocean — vast, nurturing, capable of drowning you. Protection that doesn't ask permission. | A character who protects *everyone*, whether they want it or not. Smothering as love. The ocean doesn't negotiate with swimmers. |
| **Orunmila** | Wisdom, divination, destiny | Knowledge of *consequence*. Orunmila doesn't predict the future — he reads the *weight* of choices. Ifa divination is about understanding what your actions *set in motion*. | A character who sees consequence chains. Not a prophet — a *reader* of cause and effect. They won't tell you what will happen; they'll tell you what you're *already doing* to yourself. |

**Using orisha logic:** The key insight is *moral complexity without contradiction*. Oshun isn't "good but has a dark side" — she's *one thing* that contains both. Write characters whose opposing qualities aren't in conflict; they're *the same quality* seen from different angles.

### 5.4 Slavic tradition

Slavic folklore is *domestic*. The spirits live in your house, your bathhouse, your barn. The horror isn't cosmic — it's that the familiar has turned.

| Being | Core nature | Thematic material | Character texture |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Domovoi** (house spirit) | Guardian of the household. Protects the family — but *only* that family. If you move, you must invite him. If you don't, he stays and sulks. Can be helpful or poltergeist-like depending on respect shown. | Belonging as spiritual law. Protection with *terms*. The spirit doesn't serve you; it *cohabits* with you. | A character bound to a *place*, not a cause. They protect their territory — the arena, the shrine steps, a corner of the marketplace — with absolute devotion. Outside that territory? Indifferent. The devotion is geographic, not moral. |
| **Rusalka** (water spirit) | Drowned women, often suicides or murder victims. Beautiful, sorrowful, dangerous. They didn't choose to become this. The water keeps what it takes. | Grief as transformation. The victim who became the hazard. Sorrow that drowns others. | A character who died wrong and can't let go — not from rage (that's onryo) but from *sorrow*. They pull others into their grief the way water pulls swimmers. Not malicious. Just... unable to surface. |
| **Baba Yaga** (forest witch) | Lives in a hut on chicken legs. Flies in a mortar. Can help or hinder — her response depends on *how you ask*. Tests protagonists with impossible tasks. Ambiguous: not good, not evil, but *fair* in a terrifying way. | The test as gift. Help that looks like cruelty. The crone who knows more than she says. | A character who *tests* other fighters — not to defeat them, but to see what they're made of. Her "help" is always conditional on passing the test. She won't explain the rules. The rules are the test. |
| **Leshy** (forest guardian) | Shapeshifting protector of the forest. Can be kind to the respectful, deadly to the disrespectful. Leads travelers astray — not from malice, but because the forest doesn't *want* to be navigated. | The wild as sovereign. Nature doesn't serve humans; humans are *guests*. Getting lost as spiritual instruction. | A character who *is* a place's immune system. They don't hate intruders; they *redirect* them. Fighters find themselves turned around, disoriented — not attacked, just... unable to arrive. The forest decides where you go. |

### 5.5 Chinese tradition

| Being | Core nature | Thematic material | Character texture |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Huli jing** (fox spirit) | Gains power and tails with age (nine tails = near-godhood). Can be benevolent protector or devastating seductress (Daji). Cultivation over centuries. The fox *earns* its power through time. | Time as power. Seduction as strategy. The long cultivation — power that's *grown*, not granted. | A character whose strength comes from *age* — not experience (that's human), but sheer duration. They've been cultivating for centuries. Their power isn't flashy; it's *deep*. Like the difference between a puddle and a well. |
| **Jiangshi** (hopping corpse) | Reanimated dead, stiff, hopping. Created when a soul can't leave the body — often due to improper burial. Bound by ritual: they fear mirrors, peach wood, rooster calls. | The body that won't release the soul. Improper rites as spiritual malfunction. Death *interrupted*. | A character caught between death and departure — not a ghost (soul without body) but a *body without proper release*. They move wrong because the transition was wrong. The tragedy isn't death; it's the *botched* death. Ritual could free them. No one knows the right ritual. |
| **Xiezhi** (justice beast) | Single-horned beast that can distinguish truth from lies. Gores liars with its horn. Symbol of justice in imperial China. | Truth as *physical* force. Lies have a shape the xiezhi can see. Justice isn't debated — it's *gored*. | A character who *cannot* tolerate lies — not morally, but *physically*. Falsehood causes them pain. They don't argue with liars; they *react*. Like an allergy to untruth. This makes them terrible at politics and devastating in person. |
| **Jingwei** (the determined bird) | A drowned girl reincarnated as a bird, eternally trying to fill the sea with pebbles. Futile. Unstoppable. She will never succeed. She will never stop. | Futility as devotion. The task that can't be completed and won't be abandoned. | A character pursuing something impossible — not from delusion, but from *commitment*. They know it can't be done. They're doing it anyway. This isn't stubbornness; it's a different definition of meaning. The pebbles matter because she carries them. |

### 5.6 Hindu and Buddhist traditions

| Being | Core nature | Thematic material | Character texture |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Rakshasa** | Shapechanging demons, often powerful and intelligent. Not mindless evil — many are kings, scholars, strategists. Ravana (Ramayana) was a rakshasa *and* a great devotee of Shiva. Complexity is the point. | Power with devotion. The demon who prays. Intelligence in service of appetite. | A character who is genuinely *devout* — to what, unclear — and genuinely dangerous. | **Naga** | Serpent beings. Guardians of water, treasure, and esoteric knowledge. Can be benevolent or wrathful. Associated with transformation (shedding skin). | Knowledge as *coiled* thing. ### 5.7 Norse tradition

| Being | Core nature | Thematic material | Character texture |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Draugr** (undead) | Corpse that refuses to stay buried. Guards its burial mound and treasure. Physically powerful, sometimes magically potent. Not mindless — *purposeful*. It has a *job*: guard. | Duty beyond death. The task that outlasts the life. Possession as *responsibility*. | A character still performing a duty from a life they can't fully remember. They guard *something* — a place, a person, an idea. The Reincarnation gives them new faces but the duty persists through every death. They don't know why they guard. They just do. |
| **Völva** (seeress) | Practitioner of seidr (fate-magic). Respected and feared. Sees the threads of fate but cannot change them — only *speak* them. Odin himself consulted völur. | Knowledge as burden. Seeing without power to act. The prophecy that *must* be spoken. | A character who *knows* things about other fighters' fates — and is compelled to speak them. Not from cruelty; from the nature of the knowing. The knowledge *wants* to be said. This makes them isolated: no one wants to hear their own ending. |
| **Berserker** | Warrior who enters a trance-fury (possibly ritual, possibly pharmacological). Fights without armor, without fear, without *self*. The fury is sacred — dedicated to Odin. | Ecstasy as combat. The self *dissolved* in battle. Violence as religious experience. | A character for whom fighting is *sacred* — not metaphorically. The moment of combat is the moment they're closest to the divine. Outside the ring, they're gentle, even shy. The contrast isn't a twist; it's the *theology*. |
| **Nornir** (fates) | Three beings who tend Yggdrasil and weave the threads of fate. Not gods — *older* than gods. They don't decide fate; they *maintain* it. Even gods are downstream of the weavers. | A character who *tends* something — the shrine, the trial's rules, the Reincarnation mechanism itself. Not powerful in the fighting sense; powerful in the *infrastructural* sense. If they stopped tending, things would... fray. |

### 5.8 Mesoamerican tradition

| Being | Core nature | Thematic material | Character texture |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Nahual** (shapeshifter) | A person's animal counterpart / alternate form. Not a curse — a *companion self*. In Mexica belief, everyone has a nahual. Powerful practitioners can shift at will. The animal is not separate; it's *you*, otherwise. | The self as *plural*. You are not one thing. The animal isn't a transformation — it's a *truth*. | A character with an animal nature that's not a power but an *identity*. They don't "turn into" the animal; they *are* the animal, also. The human form is just the one that talks. (Menajerii has nahual-adjacent energy, filtered through the spirit-walker frame.) |
| **Alebrije** (spirit guide) | Brightly colored composite creatures from Oaxacan tradition. Guides for the soul's journey. Each one is unique — a personal psychopomp. Modern, but rooted in dream-vision logic. | Guidance as *art*. The soul's companion is beautiful, strange, and *yours*. No two are alike because no two souls are alike. | A character accompanied by a spirit-guide that's *visually* impossible — colors that don't exist, shapes that shouldn't work. Not a pet; a *counterpart*. It doesn't fight for them; it *witnesses* them. Like the waki in Noh. |
| **Quetzalcoatl** (feathered serpent) | Creator god, bringer of maize, patron of knowledge and wind. Sacrificed himself to create humanity. Stole bones from the underworld to make people. A god who *descends*. | The god who goes *down*. Creation through sacrifice. Knowledge as something *stolen* from death. | A character who *descended* — from a higher state, a better place, a fuller self — to be here. Not fallen (that's punishment); *descended* (that's choice). They gave something up to enter the trial. What? They won't say. The sacrifice is the character. |
| **Mictlantecuhtli** (lord of the dead) | Ruler of Mictlan (the underworld). Not evil — *necessary*. The dead need a lord. Depicted as a skeletal figure, often with owls. presides over the inevitable with complete calm. | Death as *administration*. Not tragedy, not punishment — *process*. The dead are not lost; they're *filed*. | A character who treats death with bureaucratic calm. Not cold — *orderly*. They've seen every Reincarnation. They keep count. The trial's deaths don't upset them because death isn't the end of the process; it's *step three*. |

### 5.9 How to use this sourcebook (the anti-costume rule)

**The rule:** Borrow the *logic*, never the *look*.

❌ **Costume:** "She's a kitsune, so she has fox ears, nine tails, and tricks people."
✅ **Logic:** "She operates on kitsune logic — her kindness might be a centuries-long strategy, or her cruelty might be protection. The reader is never sure which. No fox ears. Just uncertain motives."

❌ **Costume:** "He's a wendigo, so he's a starving ice monster who eats people."
✅ **Logic:** "He operates on wendigo logic — his wanting has become his nature. He remembers when enough was enough. The horror is the memory, not the hunger."

**The test:** Remove every mythological proper noun from the bio. Does the character still work? If yes, the mythology is *texturing* the character (good). If the character collapses without the name-drop, the mythology was a *costume* (bad).

**The rhyme rule** (from NORTH-STAR.md): "The layers land where they already rhyme." Don't staple a wendigo onto a character who has no hunger. Don't give a character kappa logic if they have no code. The folkloric layer must *rhyme* with what's already there — it should feel like recognition, not decoration.

## Closing: The One-Paragraph Version

If you remember nothing else:

**Three dimensions, not one trait. State, don't explain. No American slang. Short paradoxical quotes. Relationships that reveal character AND cosmology. Body/Mind/Soul in friction. Always a gap between what they believe and what's true. Purification as removal, not punishment. One concrete signature image. Ground every proper noun in canon. Borrow folkloric logic, never costumes. Write as the witness, not the explainer. Trust the reader. Leave space.**

The universe is already strange, specific, and deep. Your job isn't to invent strangeness — it's to *notice* the strangeness that's already in CANON.md and write characters who belong inside it.

*Fight. Die. Return. Claim the forces. Forge the katana. Become whole.*
